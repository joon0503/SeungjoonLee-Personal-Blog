## Introduction

The camera pose series started from the point where we already have an image (specifically a RGB image), and I mentioned I will write a separate post on the lower-level parts of the camera. This is the post that dives into the low-level details such as camera sensors, image processing by ISP and pixel formats. These topics are genuinely interesting by themselves, so I did spent quite a bit of time digging into the technical details. However, as a robotics engineer, these knowledge have helped me on various occasions such as choosing gain and exposure, fixing "colors look wrong" issues, and picking a pixel format such that data fits on USB bandwidth. These decisions are becoming more important since camera images are essential part of the modern learning-based approach, and how image looks like may change how traning and inference is done, and ultimately whether task succeeds or fails.

In this post, I will focus on the process that happens between photon hitting a sensor, and us getting bytes. How those bytes affect downstream tasks will be covered in the future.

### Exposure Time

{/* TODO
  - How long the photosites integrate light. Doubling it doubles the signal ("one stop").
  - Trade-offs: motion blur, maximum frame rate (exposure ≤ frame period), flicker under mains-powered lighting
    (50/60 Hz → exposure in multiples of 10 ms / 8.33 ms).
  - Auto exposure (AE): what it optimizes, and why you often want to lock it for vision algorithms.
*/}

### Gain and ISO

{/* TODO
  - Analog gain (amplifier before the ADC) vs digital gain (multiplication after it). Units: dB vs ×. ISO as the
    photographic equivalent.
  - Why analog gain is preferred: it amplifies the signal before the ADC's own noise and quantization are added,
    so those shrink relative to the signal. Digital gain multiplies the quantized numbers: it cannot add
    information, it skips codes (fewer effective bits) and is equivalent to brightening in post-processing.
    Typical sensors use analog gain up to some limit (e.g. 16×), then switch to digital.
  - Gain does not add light: it amplifies signal and noise together, and reduces headroom (clipping).
    A good visual explanation of what ISO does (and does not) change [@minutephysics-iso].
  - Same brightness from long exposure + low gain vs short exposure + high gain: blur vs noise.
*/}

## Seeing Color: The Bayer Pattern

{/* TODO
  - A color filter array (CFA) over the photosites: each pixel measures only one of R, G, B [@bayer1976].
  - Why twice as many green pixels: the eye's luminance sensitivity peaks in green, so green carries most of the
    detail.
  - The four orderings RGGB, BGGR, GRBG, GBRG: the name is the top-left 2 × 2 tile. Flipping or cropping the
    image by one pixel changes the pattern (a common source of bugs).

### Raw Formats

{/* TODO
  - What a "raw" frame is: one 8/10/12-bit value per pixel, no color yet.
  - Packing: RAW8, RAW10/RAW12 unpacked in 16-bit containers vs packed (e.g. MIPI CSI-2 RAW10: 4 pixels in 5 bytes).
  - V4L2 names: SRGGB10, SBGGR8, … [@v4l2-pixfmt].
  - Real example: the Sigma fp frame used in the figures stores 8-bit codes, and the DNG's linearization table
    expands them to 12 bits (black level 256, white 4095). The table is piecewise linear: steps of 4 DN in the
    shadows, 32–73 DN in the highlights. Same idea as gamma, and as the PWL companding of HDR automotive /
    machine-vision sensors: spend the codes where shot noise is small.
*/}

### White Balance

{/* TODO
  - The same white object looks different under tungsten (~2700 K) and daylight (~6500 K) light. The eye adapts;
    the sensor does not.
  - White balance = per-channel gains (usually G fixed at 1, scale R and B), applied on raw data.
  - Auto white balance (AWB): gray-world, white-patch, and more; why it fails on scenes dominated by one color.
  - Why to fix white balance (and exposure) for vision and data collection: consistency across frames.
*/}

## From RGB to YUV

{/* TODO
  - Why separate brightness from color: compatibility with black-and-white TV historically; today, because the
    eye is much more sensitive to luminance detail than to chroma detail, which enables chroma subsampling.
  - Y′CbCr is computed from gamma-encoded R′G′B′ (the prime); "YUV" is the common, loose name.
  - The conversion: Y′ = K_R R′ + K_G G′ + K_B B′, Cb and Cr as scaled B′ − Y′ and R′ − Y′.
    BT.601 (K_R = 0.299, K_B = 0.114) vs BT.709 (K_R = 0.2126, K_B = 0.0722) [@bt601; @bt709].
*/}

### Full Range vs Limited Range

{/* TODO
  - Limited ("TV", "video", "studio") range: Y′ ∈ [16, 235], Cb/Cr ∈ [16, 240] for 8 bit; full ("PC", "JPEG")
    range uses [0, 255]. Origin: headroom/footroom for analog over/undershoot.
  - Symptoms of a mismatch: washed-out image (limited decoded as full) and crushed blacks/clipped whites (full
    decoded as limited). Matrix mismatch (601 vs 709): subtle hue shifts in saturated reds and greens.
  - Where the flag lives: V4L2 quantization / colorspace fields, FFmpeg's `-color_range`, JPEG (always full),
    and what OpenCV's cvtColor assumes.
*/}
