## Introduction

The camera pose series started from the point where we already have an image (specifically a RGB image), and I mentioned I will write a separate post on the lower-level parts of the camera. This is the post that dives into the low-level details such as camera sensors, image processing by ISP and pixel formats. These topics are genuinely interesting by themselves, so I did spent quite a bit of time digging into the technical details. However, as a robotics engineer, these knowledge have helped me on various occasions such as choosing gain and exposure, fixing "colors look wrong" (or "colors look washed out") issues, and picking a pixel format such that data fits on USB bandwidth. These decisions are becoming more important since camera images are essential part of the modern learning-based approach, and how image looks like may change (with decisions *unknowningly* made by you or by camera) how traning and inference is done. This may determine whether task succeeds or fails.

In this post, I will focus on the process that happens between photon hitting a sensor, and us getting bytes. How those bytes affect downstream tasks will be covered in the future.

## The Sensor: Turning Light into Numbers
A *sensor* is the chip with a grid of photosites where each converts photons into electrons and ultimately into a voltage that a circuit can read. Having the mental model of how sensor works have helped me understand how various aspects of the sensor affects the image it produces. Let me go through some of these one by one.

### Global and Rolling Shutter

Next step in the lense that we may consider as roboics engineer is whether camera is *global* or *rolling shutter*. Briefly speaking, global shutter is when lights are captured at the same moment for all pixel of the sensor. In rolling shutter, lights are caputred for each row, sensor values are read out, and then moves onto the next row. The difference between the first and last row is the *readout time*, which may span few to tends of milliseconds. Most importantly, this means that each row of an image is captured at slightly difference time. This may create a weird distortion when object is moving, which can be simulated in the diagram below. For real footages and excellent explanation of how it works can also be found in [@smartereveryday-rolling-shutter].

For robotics application, this means that the image you are getting (and feeding to the policy) may be distorted, and distorted diffrently depending on how the object is moving. Technically speaking, this also means if your policy is deployed on a new hardware with different characteristics, it may produce different image that is different from the one you saw during training. However, its impact on the overall training pipeline (such as VLA) should be minimal since objects aren't moving fast enough. 

The distortion from the rolling shutter becomes bigger problem if you are working with visual SLAM or marker-based estimation. Here, accuracy is determined by the visual feature (and strong prior on the shape of marker), and even slight distortion may affect the accuracy of the estimated pose.

{/* TODO
  - Rolling shutter: rows are reset and read out one after another, so each row is exposed over a slightly

  - Artifacts of a rolling shutter under motion: skew (leaning verticals when panning), wobble ("jello" from

### Exposure Time

*Exposure time* is how long each photosites integrate the light to produce the voltage output. Doubling the exposure time literally doubles the photons it captures, and hence the voltage signal. Practically speaking, exposure time is probably the first setting you can tune from the software. If exposure time is too small, image will be dark (since there's less photons captured). Therefore, the usual goal is to increase the exposure time to obtain a bright image. However, it comes with the following trade-offs.

#### Maximum Frame Rate

The theoretical upper limit of the exposure time if often capped by the maximum frame rate. Camera has to capture an image, read and process it, and send it the host. This process is repeated for each frame it generates. Therefore, it is obvious that exposure time cannot be longer than the desired (or supported) frame rate. Oftentimes, cameras support 30hz to 60hz, leading to upper bound of 33.3ms or 16.6ms for the exposure time. In practice, we have to factor in the read-out time from the sensor (which includes additional operations such as reading the photosites value, ISP processing) which limits the maximum exposure time to be around 30ms or 15ms.

#### Motion Blur

Now, with maximum frame rate given, you may decide to choose maximum exposure time to be 30ms (for 30 fps) right away. However, a second limiting factor is the *motion blur*. Recall that photosites integrates the photons it receives during the exposure time. The photons that hits the sensors are from the same *ray* of the light. Now imagine an object moving pass the ray. During first few moment, photons will not be from an object, and sensor will integrate photons from the background. Once the object enters, sensors will not integrate the photons from the object. If the object leaves the ray, sensor will integrate the background again. Compared this to the ray where object was fixed throughout the expsoure time. Both sensor will *see* the object, but the one with moving object will have less intensity of the object. On the image, this shows up as motion blur, where object seems to be smeared across pixel instead of being a sharp snapshot.

Given the principle of the motion blur, now we can see that the followings affect its severity: i) exposure time, ii) speed of the object. If object is moving fast, it requires lower exposure to limit the effect of the motion blur.

#### Saturation 

Long exposure also may cause the *saturation* issue. If exposure is too long, and there's too much light on the sensor, its circuitary becomes saturated. Its impact on the resulting image is that image contains pixels that are at the highest brightness, and it cannot distinguish two pixels which reached saturation. This means image loses details and causes issues such as missing an edge or an object.

In practice, I never faced the saturation issue. For many robotics application, the scene is usually well-lit so the light is relatively uniform, and you can always reduce the exposure if this happens.

#### Flickering 

Perhaps a bigger constraint on the exposure time for robotics application is the *flickering*. Flickering is visual artifact in the video where we see either a bands of varying brightness across the image (for rolling shutter) or changing brightness of the whole image throughout the video. This happens because of how modern artificial lighting (e.g., standard LEDs) work.

Although the modern lighting seems to have a constant brightness, in reality its brightness oscillates due to the usage of AC power. Depending on the frequency of the AC power, its light oscillates with twice the frequency. For example, lights installed in the region with 60hz AC power will have light pulses at 120hz (which is period of 8.33ms). Then the brightness of an image can be found as the integrate of the light across its exposure time.

### Gain and ISO

{/* TODO
  - Analog gain (amplifier before the ADC) vs digital gain (multiplication after it). Units: dB vs ×. ISO as the
    photographic equivalent.
  - Why analog gain is preferred: it amplifies the signal before the ADC's own noise and quantization are added,
    so those shrink relative to the signal. Digital gain multiplies the quantized numbers: it cannot add
    information, it skips codes (fewer effective bits) and is equivalent to brightening in post-processing.
    Typical sensors use analog gain up to some limit (e.g. 16×), then switch to digital.
  - Gain does not add light: it amplifies signal and noise together, and reduces headroom (clipping).
    A good visual explanation of what ISO does (and does not) change [@minutephysics-iso].
  - Same brightness from long exposure + low gain vs short exposure + high gain: blur vs noise.
*/}
